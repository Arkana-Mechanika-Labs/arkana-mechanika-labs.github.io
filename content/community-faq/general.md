## 3.1 Character Generation

Truly an innovation when compared with the crop of weeds that are D&D adventure RPGs. The system for creating characters to populate the party is far more realistic and fluid than arbitrarily assigning points for strength, constitution, dexterity, etc.

Players can create characters and guide them literally from infancy to adventuring age, whether that age be 20, 40, or 60. Players choose the initial physical characteristics, but they can also determine the skill sets that the characters acquire as they work at various vocations. For instance, life-long soldiers will gain great skill in several weapons types but learn little, if any, alchemical skill. Likewise, monks and nuns tend to grow higher ratings in Virtue, Speak Latin, and Religion but end up with little Stealth or Artifice. Of course, players can create characters that tend to be "jacks-of-all-trades" yet masters of none. The choice is truly up to the player how to mold and shape their creation.

Just look at the changes in attributes as you browse each occupation or family background, and you will get all the hints you need to make a good choice.

The second party member is traditionally the strongest among the party. This fellow could possibly be a backup alchemist, although you would have to increase his intelligence. My favorite choice is to make him the perceptive one as well as the strongest.

The third member is usually the alchemist. Increase his intelligence to at least 35 while maintaining strength and endurance > 30.

The fourth member sometimes ends up being almost as strong as the second member. I like to give this member as high a value for healing as he can get along with high religion.

You may or may not want to add a fifth character depending on whether or not you feel it's cheating. If you create a fifth character and let him grow to a ripe old age, he will bring to the party many wonderful (expensive) potions. Transfer these potions to your leader and retire the old rascal. ### What do attributes and skills cost?

Childhood gives 89 to 97 experience points, depending on the family background (Rural Commoners give the most, Nobility the fewest), to spend on attributes. Each later five-year occupation gives its own points for skills: 12 to 31, plus 20 for the first adult term and 5 for the second. The first adult term also adds 2 to every skill.

<p class="dl-formula">1 point per step, 2 from 29, 3 from 39 (attributes)<br><small>1 point per step, 2 from 49, 3 from 79 (skills)</small></p>

Points can't take an attribute past 40, or a skill past 89. Each occupation also limits how many points a term can put into each of its skills: a Soldier can add at most 5 to Edged Weapons in one term, a Thief up to 9 to Stealth. Occupations change attributes on their own as well, by a point or two each term, so an attribute can end above 40 that way.

A new man starts with Endurance 12, Strength 15 and Charisma 11, a new woman with Endurance 14, Strength 12 and Charisma 12; all other attributes start at 12.

### Which saints favour women?

St. Agnes can't be prayed to at all unless an able woman is in the party. St. Agatha raises a woman's Endurance by 12 to 23 for a day, a man's by 6 to 11. St. John of Bridlington restores all of a woman's lost Endurance and a fifth of her lost Strength and adds a boost on top; a man gets back only 30% of his Endurance and 10% of his Strength. St. Margaret is far more generous to women as well.

### How old should my characters be?

The first fifteen years are always childhood, and every occupation after that takes five, so the youngest adventurer is 20. You can't start another term after 65, so the oldest is 70. Every term ending at 35 or later costs attributes, and the losses grow with age:

| Term ending at | 35 | 40 | 45 | 50 | 55 | 60 | 65 | 70 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Endurance | −1 | −2 | −2 | −3 | −3 | −3 | −5 | −6 |
| Strength | −1 | −1 | −2 | −2 | −2 | −3 | −4 | −6 |
| Agility | −1 | −2 | −2 | −2 | −3 | −3 | −4 | −4 |
| Perception | | | | −1 | −1 | −2 | −3 | −3 |
| Intelligence | | | | | | −1 | −2 | −2 |
| Charisma | | | | −1 | −1 | −2 | −3 | −3 |

The losses add up: a character who stops at 60 has lost 14 Endurance, 11 Strength and 13 Agility over his career. On top of that, once a character is past 40, an occupation no longer raises (or lowers) Endurance or Strength. So more terms buy more skill at the price of the body. Age only matters here: once the adventure begins, birthdays change nothing (see [3.15](#315-retiring-characters)).

### How do I become a Hermit, a Master Alchemist or a Knight?

Each occupation has its own conditions on age, attributes, skills, sex, family and earlier careers, and the list shows at most 15 choices. Some of the rarer ones:

- **Hermit:** Virtue of 15 or more. Nothing else.
- **Alchemist:** Intelligence 30, and a past as Priest, Bishop, Abbot, Student, Clerk, Professor or Alchemist (or the last two terms as Oblate, Monk, Friar or Physician).
- **Master Alchemist:** Intelligence 35 and at least one term as Alchemist.
- **Physician:** Healing 15, and a past as Student, Clerk, Professor, Alchemist or Physician.
- **Knight:** Virtue over 15, and a noble youth, or a past as Manorial Lord or Knight, or a last term as Noble Heir, Courtier, Bishop, Abbot or Captain.
- **Priest, Bishop and Friar** are open to men only. Bishop needs Intelligence, Perception and Charisma of 25 and a last term as Bishop or Abbot (or the last two as Manorial Lord, Priest or Courtier).

### What does a new character bring?

A weapon for his best weapon skill, whatever kit his last occupation gave (soldiers, nobles and craftsmen get armour or weapons), any saints a clerical career taught him, and one quality-25 potion of each kind he learned a formula for. Only the Oblate, Professor, Alchemist and Master Alchemist learn formulas, one per term (two for a Master Alchemist), so those are the old rascals with potions in their packs.

### Should I build my own party or take the ready-made one?

The ready-made party (Gretchen, Gunther, Hans and Ebhard) is four experienced adventurers aged 35 to 45, with ordinary 25q weapons and armour: a good way to start playing at once. A party you build yourself can be shaped to your plans, and young characters still have their best attributes ahead of the aging table. Neither choice locks you in: characters can be retired at any inn and replaced.

From the manual:

> Money is counted in three denominations: gold florins, silver groschen, and silver pfennigs. Because distribution of wealth and wages was so different, comparisons to modern currency is impossible. In general, florins were very valuable, but are used only for large transactions; groschen are fairly valuable and are in daily use; pfennigs are "small change," important only to the very poor.
>
> Conversion between the denominations follows the classic medieval pattern of "a dozen and a gross," namely:
>
> 12 pfennigs (pf) = 1 groschen (gr)\
> 20 groschen (gr) = 1 florin (fl)\
> 240 pfennigs (pf) = 1 florin (fl)

### What do shops charge, and what do they pay?

A shop asks twice an item's list value at quality 25, and proportionally more for better quality. It pays a quarter of what it would charge: half the list value for a 25q item. Big cities sell common goods a little cheaper and rare ones a little dearer. The party leader's Charisma moves both prices by 1% per point away from 25: a leader with Charisma 40 buys 15% cheaper and sells 15% dearer. Good standing in the town and fame make no difference; only bad standing costs you, up to 9%.

At Charisma 85 the two prices meet, and above it a shop pays more for an item than it charges. St. Denis raises Charisma by 15 to 44 for a day, which is how players found the "buy low, sell high" trick.

### I'm broke and nobody will hire me. Am I stuck?

No. A new party starts with only 8 to 10 groschen, and some patience is normal. While the party stays at an inn, each member can "earn a little money": the game finds the best-paid work for that member's skills, from beggar or messenger to clerk, craftsman or courtier, and it pays at least 2 pfennigs a day. That rarely makes anyone rich. Meanwhile:

- Fight the thieves and bandits who come for you, and sell what they carry. Cash found on bodies is small change: at most 10 groschen and 10 pfennigs per find.
- Take the town council's errands and commissions (see [3.9](#39-getting-jobs)).
- Sell loot and spare potions; a shop pays a quarter of its asking price, as above.

## 3.3 Alchemy

{{< plate src="/images/faq/alchemist-laboratory.png" alt="An alchemist's laboratory: flasks on a shelf, a hanging lamp, a table with vessels" side="right" pixel="true" >}}
An alchemist's laboratory. Scene picture from the game (MicroProse, 1992).
{{< /plate >}}

One of the many aspects of Darklands that sets it apart from cookie-cutter D&D games is its use of Alchemy, as opposed to magic. With alchemical formulae and their corresponding ingredients, characters can create a variety of potions. The formulae must be learned and the ingredients procured, however. They don't just fall into your lap. Based upon your character's Intelligence and Alchemy skill, the quality of your philosopher's stone, and some good old-fashioned luck, you'll be creating potions in no time. Options to create potions appear when the party has set up camp or taken residence in a city/village inn. From there the character can choose to concoct potions that he has learned.

### How good will my potions be?

{{< plate src="/images/faq/brunschwig.jpg" alt="Woodcut of two men distilling with furnaces and alembics" side="right" size="small" >}}
Distilling, from Hieronymus Brunschwig's *Liber de arte distillandi* (Strassburg, 1512). Science History Institute, via Wikimedia Commons, public domain.
{{< /plate >}}

Each potion has three formulas, named after different alchemists (Galen's, Michael Scot's and Avicenna's Essence o'Grace, for example). A potion brewed from the first formula is quality 25, from the second 35, from the third 45. The later formulas are harder to brew, but they often need fewer ingredients: Avicenna's Essence o'Grace takes five units of ingredients against Galen's nine. Potions bought from an alchemist are always 25.

Quality matters most for Essence o'Grace. It gives back twice its quality in percent of the Strength the drinker has lost: a 25q potion restores half, a 45q potion nine tenths. It also raises Endurance for about 34 hours, by 9 to 14 points at 25q and up to 22 to 29 at 45q. Don't drink it while an Ironarm potion or a saint is holding your Strength above its maximum: the Essence pulls it back down.

### What are my chances of brewing a potion?

<p class="dl-formula">Chance (%) = 100 + Intelligence + Alchemy + philosopher's stone − the formula's difficulty<br><small>at most 99, for the alchemist who brews</small></p>

The difficulty (the formula's "mystic number") runs from 80 for al-Razi's Noxious Aroma to 210 for Nicolaus of A's Transformation; the Essence o'Grace formulas are among the easiest, 81 to 98. All potions of one formula brewed at the same time succeed or fail together. Up to three at once, the chance is as above; for more, the 100 is divided: 50 for four or five, 33 for six or seven, 25 for eight or nine. So a less skilled alchemist should brew in batches of three.

A failed brew loses the ingredients. In a town or village, half the time it also causes an accident, and the inn or lodging will not have the party back for 30 days, or 120 days if the brew exploded. Brewing in camp in the wilderness carries no such risk. The game rates formulas as low, medium and high risk, but because of an original bug it works backwards: low-risk formulas explode in three failures out of four, high-risk ones in half.

Success and failure both train the Alchemy skill. Brews finish the next time time passes; if you leave the camp first, the brew is lost.

### Why can't I buy Essence o'Grace, Stone-tar or Transformation formulas?

{{< plate src="/images/faq/city-alchemist.png" alt="An old alchemist writing at his desk among flasks" side="right" pixel="true" >}}
An alchemist at his desk. Scene picture from the game (MicroProse, 1992).
{{< /plate >}}

Because of an original bug, the alchemists who sell formulas only ever offer the first 22 of the 66: the three formulas of Noxious Aroma, Eyeburn, Black Cloud, Fleadust, Eater Water, Breath of Death and Sunburst, and al-Tamimi's Thunderbolt. Each town offers four of them, for 10, 20 or 30 florins (first, second or third formula) divided by the town's size. Every other formula has to come from elsewhere:

- **The formula exchange.** A town's best alchemist sees the party only by day, and only if your best alchemist's skill impresses him (Alchemy 30 or better always does). Despite the name he takes nothing in exchange. In four towns out of five he wants your best Alchemy skill above a figure between 30 and 79, which is fixed for each town. If he agrees, he teaches the party's leader one formula at random, usually of a potion the leader doesn't yet know. So make your alchemist the leader before you ask.
- **The forest witch.** Beaten and at your mercy, she offers three formulas for her life. They are three random picks given to each of the first four party members, and because of an original bug most picks miss: they land on another member, or teach a saint instead. **Never take her "unnatural strength"**: it kills your leader on the spot.
- Your starting characters' formulas, and rewards from some quests.

### Where do I find the ingredients?

Herbalists' stalls in market squares, university shops, physicians, the shops of mining villages, and the alchemists who don't sell potions sell them. The four bases are common everywhere, and Brimstone and Pure Gold nearly so. Nikel, Solanaceae, Aqua Regia, Camomile, Gum, Mondragora, Alum, Zinken and Pitchblende are rare; Zincblende and Antimoni rarer; Orpiment, White Cinnabar and Eastern Black Bean rarer still; Manganes and Naptha very rare; and Marsh Vapor, needed for Breath of Death, the rarest of all. Big cities stock rare goods far more often than small towns.

A shop's stock is fixed for the whole game: if a town has no Camomile today, it never will, so don't wait for it. Go to another town.

Ingredients don't need to be carried by the alchemist. A brew takes what it needs from the whole party, the alchemist's own packs first.

### What does the philosopher's stone do?

Its quality, from 1 to 25, adds straight to the chance of every brew, and it belongs to the party, not to one character. A town's best alchemist sells a better one if his is better than yours. His stone's quality is fixed for each town, so it pays to ask in several towns.

### Using the weapon and armour potions

Use them from the character sheet (press P on the potion in the inventory). The effect goes to the character, not to a particular weapon or piece of armour. Because of original bugs, several last much longer than the manual says:

- **Deadly Blade, Strongedge and Great Power** never wear off, and further doses add up. After three or four doses the counter overflows, and the bonus can fall back to almost nothing. Great Power adds to damage and penetration rather than to the weapon's quality.
- **Hardarmor** never wears off either. It adds one to three levels of armour thickness (by quality) and no quality.
- **Firewall** gives fire resistance of 50, 70 or 90 (by quality) and never wears off. Through the same bug it also counts as extra armour.
- **Trueflight, Truesight, New-wind, Ironarm and Quickmove** last about a day, as the manual says.

Drinking Transformation does nothing but add one florin to your purse: it is meant to be used in particular places, such as an evil altar or the quicksand of a peat bog.

## 3.4 Saints

Monks at prayer. Scene picture from the game (MicroProse, 1992).

{{< contributor "C. Michel Boucher" >}}
Saints can assist you at various times such as dealing with problems during travelling and also during combat. In the first instance, you can invoke the assistance of a saint to modify the outcome of a potential encounter BEFORE it happens, for example with refugees on the road. Prayer can reveal to you whether there is a trap, giving you the option to avoid contact altogether, should you not wish to involve yourself in combat.
{{< /contributor >}}

{{< contributor "Dennis Ahr" >}}
The party needs to have knowledge of certain saints in order to get along comfortably in Medieval Germany. In general, I try to visit the Monks in each town in order to check out the saints available for study. Many saints temporarily increase strength, endurance, weapons skills, armor value, perception, etc. Reading the saint's biography will advise you of the improvements. There are a couple of saints that actually are bad influences; Giles of Portugal is one I can think of. He will cost strength and permanently decrease Virtue.
{{< /contributor >}}

St. Giles of Portugal is not a bad saint, in fact. Each prayer to him costs a point of current Strength, which rest restores, and a point of Virtue, which stays lost. In return he raises Alchemy by 40 to 59, Artifice by 10 to 19, Intelligence by 5 to 9 and Perception by 15 to 24 for a day: a saint for an alchemist.

{{< contributor "C. Michel Boucher" >}}
The information given on each saint is fairly accurate when dealing with game information. Unfortunately, you can't read that until you've learned about the saint. In the Basic setting, it isn't necessary to read them carefully as the game will give you the list of saints for each situation, but in the Expert mode, you must be aware of the characteristics of the saints from reading their blurbs. The info provided in the manual is not really enough, although it does give you the level of virtue required.
{{< /contributor >}}

For about forty saints, though, what the prayer does differs from the text (see below). At Standard the game lists only the saints your characters know, as at Expert.

Savcho Savchev wrote a Flash tool for determining which saints to use in a specific situation; Flash no longer runs in today's browsers. The [saints page](/formats/world-data/saints/) lists every saint.

### What are my chances when I pray?

<p class="dl-formula">Chance (%) = (Virtue − the saint's minimum Virtue) ÷ 2 + the saint's base chance + Divine Favor offered − the saint's minimum favour<br><small>0 if Virtue or the favour offered is below the saint's minimum</small></p>

The prayer screen starts at the saint's minimum favour; offer more and the chance rises point for point, up to 99, which never fails. The favour offered is spent whether the prayer works or not. When a prayer with a chance above 66 fails, there is a further risk of losing half the offer again.

The difficulty level changes what the game tells you, not the odds. At Basic every situation's saint option is open and the chance is shown in percent; at Standard it is shown in words ("poor", "good", "superb"); at Expert it isn't shown. At Basic the leader also sees, greyed out, the saints a situation would accept even if nobody knows them.

### How do I get Divine Favor back?

A little comes back every night: Religion divided by about 20, between 1 and 5 points. A member who spends a day in camp or at an inn praying regains (Religion + Virtue) ÷ 12 + 1 points that day. Many encounters give or take favour as well: helping pilgrims or a merchant caravan gives some, and evil choices take it away.

### How do I raise Virtue?

Virtue is a skill, and like other skills it grows with use: helping pilgrims and refugees on the road, purifying a crypt, standing up to evil. Some victories give it outright: breaking up the High Sabbat gives every member +3. Evil deeds take it away: fighting the Church's men on the road costs every member 2 Virtue (and 25 Divine Favor), whatever the outcome. Each prayer to St. Giles of Portugal costs 1.

### Where can I learn saints?

- **Church and monastery libraries.** For a gift (one groschen, plus one more for every 1½ florins in your purse, at most 20 groschen), one member studies until evening and learns one of the four saints the library offers. Each place's four saints are fixed for the game, so different towns teach different saints.
- **Hermits** in the wilderness can teach a saint.
- **A cathedral** can teach the leader, and a city lord may reward you with one.
- **Clerical careers** teach random saints while you create a character: one per term as Priest, Friar, Hermit or Monk/Nun, two as Bishop or Abbot.

### Why is the saint option greyed out?

At Standard and Expert, an encounter's "call on a saint" line opens only when an able member knows one of the (up to four) saints that situation accepts. The option itself is fine; the party just doesn't know the right saints yet. A saint you do know is greyed out on the prayer screen when your Virtue is below his minimum, or you have less Divine Favor than he asks for.

### Which saints mislead?

Some saints don't do what their text says. A few examples:

- **St. George** promises better weapons and armour; he gives Strength, Agility, weapon skills and Riding, but changes no quality.
- **St. Giles** (the hermit, not Giles of Portugal) has Streetwise and Woodwise swapped: a big boost to Streetwise, a small one to Woodwise.
- **St. Hedwig** heals nothing, although the text says she does.
- **St. Agatha** gives a day's Endurance boost where the text promises healing.
- **St. Cecilia** raises standing only in the town you are in, and only if you carry a harp or a flute.
- The saints who promise resistance to fire set a mark that never wears off and counts as extra armour in every fight (an original bug).

### What does each armour do?

Armour comes in two pieces, one for the body and one for the limbs. Its value is what a weapon's penetration must beat; its weight counts against the wearer's load.

| Armour | Value | Weight (body / limbs) | List price, pf (body / limbs) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Padded | 1 | 6 / 6 | 50 / 50 |
| Leather | 1 | 4 / 4 | 75 / 75 |
| Studded Leather | 2 | 8 / 10 | 250 / 275 |
| Cuirbouilli | 2 | 6 / 8 | 350 / 350 |
| Scale | 3 | 21 / 24 | 600 / 600 |
| Brigandine | 4 | 27 / 29 | 700 / 700 |
| Chainmail | 4 | 23 / 26 | 900 / 850 |
| Plate | 5 | 28 / 31 | 1650 / 1500 |

Cuirbouilli is the light armour worth having: as good as studded leather and lighter. Brigandine protects like chainmail, costs less and weighs more.

### How much can I carry?

The weight of what a character wears and wields (armour, weapons, shield) is compared with Strength + Endurance. Up to half of that sum costs nothing. Above half, Agility counts only two thirds, and blows come more slowly; above the whole sum, Agility counts a third; above one and a half times it, Agility drops to 1. A full suit of plate (59) with a weapon already takes a character with Strength and Endurance of 30 each past the first step.

### Does quality matter?

Quality decides damage, not hitting. Whether a blow lands depends on the weapon skill, Agility, the target's guard and shield, and whether the wielder is strong and skilled enough for the weapon; the weapon's quality plays no part. When a blow lands, each 10 points of the weapon's quality above the armour's quality add one level of damage, and each 10 below take one away. A 45q sword against 25q armour hits two levels harder than a 25q one. The same goes in reverse for your armour.

### What are glancing blows and penetration?

Every weapon has a penetration, and every armour a value. If the penetration is higher, the weapon does its full damage. If they are equal, the weapon's damage bonus is halved; if the penetration is lower, it is cut to an eighth. A hit that only just succeeds, within 10 points of the needed roll, is a glancing blow and loses 1 to 4 points of penetration.

This is why the right weapon depends on the enemy's armour. Against chainmail (value 4) a short sword (penetration 4) keeps half its bonus of 8, while a long sword (penetration 3) keeps only an eighth of its 12. Some penetrations: falchion and hand axe 2; long sword, dagger and short spear 3; short sword, mace, maul, military flail, halberd and pike 4; two-hand flail 5; great hammer, bows and crossbow 6; arbalest 7.

Strength matters too. Strength above the weapon's "full strength" adds a damage level, and one more for every further 5 points; falling below its minimum Strength costs damage and makes the fighter much less likely to hit.

### Fast weapons or heavy ones?

A fighter's pace depends on the weapon's speed, his skill and his Agility. Each point of weapon speed costs four times as much time as a point of skill or Agility saves. With skill 50 and Agility 30, a dagger (speed 25) strikes about twice as often as a maul (speed 90), and a long sword (speed 55) about a third more often than the maul. A heavy weapon has to make up for that with damage and penetration.

### Are shields worth it?

Yes, as long as the character fights one-handed. In close combat a shield adds to the guard: about 7 points for a 25q small shield, 10 for a medium one, 12 for a large one, against −5 for no shield at all. Against arrows and bolts it counts for much more: 8, 16 and 24. Flails ignore shields. A character holding a two-handed weapon or a bow gets no benefit from one.

### Are missile weapons any good?

They are at their best before the melee starts. A shot's chance comes from the shooter's skill against the target's Agility (doubled) and shield. It suffers when enemies are fighting the shooter hand to hand (30% less aim for each), when the target is surrounded by the shooter's own companions, and when a cloud lies across the line. The time to aim and reload shrinks as skill and Agility grow. Bows and crossbows have high penetration (6, the arbalest 7), so a good shot goes through most armour.

### Does my equipment wear out?

Weapons never do, and ordinary blows don't wear armour either. Armour loses quality only to acid and fire: Eater Water, Thunderbolt and Arabian Fire. Armour worn down to nothing is thrown away, and any spare pieces of the same kind and quality in the pack go with it (an original bug).

In cities, adventurers can find almost anything for a price. Larger cities usually have a greater selection and higher quality of items. Cities throughout the empire are often linked by roads or waterways and citizens relied on high city walls for protection from foreign armies. Within cities, players can find many common services such as inns, armories, guild halls and the such. However, just like in real life, not every city has every service available.

{{< contributor "C. Michel Boucher" >}}
Villages lie scattered amongst the country side, consisting of a handful of families. It is in villages that you will find the least expensive housing (but no income) and possibly good blacksmithing. You can sell your acquired weapons and armour.
{{< /contributor >}}

{{< contributor "Gui Terence Ang" >}}
If you stay in a village (not a city) with the old woman, but only choose to stay overnight without selecting the "Take up residence" screen, the stay will cost you nothing. The price is never deducted from your purse. You recover only one point of lost strength, but all your endurance.
{{< /contributor >}}

She asks a pfennig per character and per horse, not 4 pfennigs, or a single pfennig in a village that likes you (standing 50 or more), and never collects it. Beware a village that has gone over to the witches: there, the night ends with the cultists attacking at midnight.

### How much is the toll at the city gate, and should I pay it?

The toll is a pfennig or two per character (the city's size divided by three, plus one). Paying is safe but teaches nothing. Talking your way past the guards depends on the leader's Charisma or Speak Common, whichever is higher, and on half your standing in the city. Success trains the leader's Speak Common and gains a point of standing. Sneaking in depends on the party's average Streetwise and Agility (reduced by what they carry). Success trains everyone's Streetwise. Once you are caught sneaking, that way is closed for twelve hours, and the guards will turn you away for a while after a failed talk.

Leaving is free. Walking out costs nothing unless the city is hostile or in unrest. Sneaking out also trains Streetwise, and only costs standing (one to four points) if you are caught.

### What does the night watch cost?

<p class="dl-formula">Fine = (1 + city size + florins you carry − standing ÷ 50) pfennigs<br><small>for each character present</small></p>

Challenged by the watch. Scene picture from the game (MicroProse, 1992).
{{< /plate >}}

The florins in your purse are part of the fine, so a rich party pays far more: four characters carrying 30 florins in a middle-sized town pay about 12 groschen. Put your money in a bank before you walk the streets at night. Paying costs an hour and nothing else. On the main street at night the watch stops you on 4 to 14 moves in 100, in the side streets on 1 to 8, more in towns with higher prices.

## 3.7 Improving Skills

{{< /plate >}}

Skill acquisition is another way in which Darklands differs from most other games, if only in the sheer number of skills that CAN be learned. Skills differ from attributes in that, while skill can change (usually improved) readily, attributes change very rarely and only then, due to supernatural causes. Skills in Darklands range from 0-99 with 0 being a total newbie in a given skill and 99 representing complete mastery.

### How do teachers work?

A teacher is hired for a week, and each pupil pays the fee for every day spent training in camp or at the inn. The university's prices are fixed: 90 pfennigs a day for Alchemy, 70 for Religion, 50 for Speak Latin, 50 for Read & Write and 110 for Healing. A monastery teaches Religion for 50, Speak Latin and Read & Write for 25. A guild master asks 50. A town's alchemist asks his own quality divided by four, plus 12 pfennigs, which is where von Lünen's 26 comes from.

Each day of training a pupil has a chance of about two thirds of the teacher's rating to gain one point: a university teacher (rated 45 to 59) gives about one day in three. A good alchemist or guild master teaches as fast or faster for a fraction of the price. No teacher stops at his own level: any of them can take a pupil to 99.

### How do the other skills go up?

By use. Talking at the gates, sneaking, healing, praying and the rest all have a small chance of a point each time they are used, larger when the attempt succeeds. Some events train the whole party at once. The closer a skill is to 100, the smaller the gains.

Weapon skills are checked once at the end of each battle: a character who hit something with his hand weapon has a chance of (100 − skill) ÷ 2 % to improve it by a few points, one who only swung it half that.

### Why did my Edged Weapons go up when I fought with a flail?

An original bug. At the end of a battle the game trains the missile skill of the weapon a character has ready. A character who threw his last javelin or throwing axe has no missile weapon left, and the game reads that empty slot as Edged Weapons. So he may improve Edged Weapons, besides his flail, axe or spear skill, instead of the skill of the weapon he threw.

## 3.8 Fame and Reputation

This abstract quality is the general point of the game. As stated by your leader in the opening dialogue, you set out to find adventure, rid the land of evil, and bring everlasting fame to your names. This rating is measured numerically and comes in two flavors: general fame and local reputation.

General fame tends to follow you around and affects your dealings with people from one end of the empire to the other. For instance, depending on your fame in doing good or evil, local lords treat you with respect or contempt.

Local reputation, however, influences your interactions within specific cities. Potential employers take your local reputation into consideration when asking for freelance jobs and city guards may or may not recognize your name when they stop you for whatever reason.

With 0 as the starting point (unknown), these attributes either rise or fall, based on your actions. The amount they change varies with the magnitude of your actions, as well.

{{< contributor "Alexander von Lünen" >}}
Some folk won't give you jobs, until your popularity has grown. While it is easier to get jobs at the Fugger, Medici and Hanse when you have high reputation, it's nearly impossible to get jobs at town halls or fortresses without such (but I pointed out previously that it might not be desirable anyway). Town leaders or fortress chiefs won't give you an audience if you're too low on reputation. Other merchants like the "everyday-items", the "foreign-trader" and the herbalist will offer jobs, too, from time to time, if you're a well-known party. Instructions for this are short: Do everything to increase your reputation, preferably by going after raubritters in their castles, and knockers in mines. These encounters occur quite often and improve your reputation by an appreciable amount.
{{< /contributor >}}

### How do fame and standing work in numbers?

Fame is one number for the whole party. Deeds add 3, 10, 25, 64 or 200 points, two thirds of that on Basic and half as much again on Expert. The Party Information screen (F6) gives a title for every 50 points, from *Unknown* to *Legendary Heroes* at 500.

Standing is kept separately for every place and runs from −99 to 99. Every change happens only with a chance of 100 minus the present standing (ignoring its sign), so the first points come easily and the last ones slowly, in both directions. The game describes it in six steps:

| Standing | The game calls you |
|---|---|
| 51 and up | a local hero |
| 11 to 50 | respected |
| −9 to 10 | unknown |
| −39 to −10 | suspected |
| −74 to −40 | wanted |
| −75 and below | hunted |

At −40 or worse the City Lord and the fortress refuse to see you at all. At −75 the city is hostile by itself; a fight with the watch also makes it hostile for some days. The Party Information screen shows your standing in any town ([3.17](#317-is-there-a-quest-log)).

### Why am I wanted? And what happens if I'm arrested?

Standing falls when you run from the night watch (a point or two) or fight it (15 to 25 points), when you are caught sneaking out of the gate, when you dig out of the town's dungeon, and after many other misdeeds. At −75 ("hunted") the town is hostile: its guards may come for you at the gates and on the quays. Fighting the night watch also makes a town hostile for a while ([3.7](#37-improving-skills)).

A party thrown into a city's dungeon loses all its money and its heavier belongings; only some small items may be left. From the cell you can pick the lock (if you still have lockpicks), climb out, dig a tunnel, let a woman in the party charm the jailer, wait for the gaoler's round, use a potion, pray, or face the magistrate. Failed attempts make the guards more watchful, and a party that is condemned risks execution. Some saints get you out: one for half your money, others at a cost in standing. A character left in a city's prison can be freed later: bribe the guards (5 florins), fight, use a potion or pray.

## 3.9 Getting Jobs

Even in 15th century Europe, people had to work to earn money that they could use to buy goods that they themselves could not provide. In Darklands, "getting a job" implies everything from staying in an inn and working for daily wages to slaying a local raubritter at the behest of the Alt Herr.

{{< contributor "C. Michel Boucher" >}}
The best way to get jobs is to visit the various interested parties in a town: Fugger, Medici, Hanseatic League, town leader, leading merchant, pawnshop owner. These will often have tasks to perform, three types in general: dealing with raubritters, recovering stolen articles, recovering lost articles. Each of these has a specific method of dealing with the problem. The amount of money you will be offered won't appear to be much but it's enough, if you keep your expenses down by travelling overland.
{{< /contributor >}}

{{< contributor "Alexander von Lünen" >}}
Success in getting jobs is only likely with the Fugger, the Medici and the Hanse in the beginning, which means it is uncertain. The merchants, the herbalist and the town leader will only offer you tasks if your reputation is high enough. Note that town leaders and Dukes in a city's fortress won't pay for the job; success only increases your local reputation, which is achieved through other jobs too (but paid more accurately ;-) ). So you'd better leave the town halls and fortresses alone and check out the marketplaces.
{{< /contributor >}}

{{< contributor "Jerry Hagen" >}}
Thanks for a well-designed, exhaustive FAQ. I did find one error...It is mentioned in the section on "getting jobs" that town leaders do not pay for the successful accomplishment of their tasks. This is incorrect. If you successfully accomplish a task for a town leader, look at your money total before and after you talk with him. You'll find that you have received between 20 and 40 florins as a reward, although the text doesn't tell you that...By the way, I'm running v483.07.
{{< /contributor >}}

Hagen is right, and only the fortress pays nothing. The City Lord pays seven florins for each point of the city's size for dealing with a raubritter (21 florins in the smallest towns, 35 in most, 56 in the largest), and three florins per point of size for an errand to fetch something. A fortress pays nothing: its commissions are recorded for the town hall, which a city with a fortress does not have. After a refusal, the City Lord won't see you for a week. A banking house that has given you a job, or that you turned down, won't see you for three days.

### The same task from several patrons?

Commissions against a raubritter always name the castle nearest to the town. Neighbouring towns share the same nearest castle, so several patrons can send you after the same robber knight.

{{< contributor "Dennis Ahr" >}}
One way to enhance the chances of getting a job is to enhance the group's local reputation with a saint. My favorite is Cecilia, because praying to her will increase the local reputation in every city. Her only requirement is that the person praying must own a musical instrument.
{{< /contributor >}}

St. Cecilia raises Charisma by 3 to 10 for a day, and, if the character praying carries a harp or a flute, raises your standing by 20 to 59 in the town you are in, not in every city (each time with the usual chance of 100 minus your standing there). Prayed to in the open country she raises no standing at all, an original bug.

## 3.11 Travelling by Land

{{< plate src="/images/faq/s3-approach.png" alt="A walled city seen at dusk across dark fields" side="right" pixel="true" >}}
Approaching a city. Scene picture from the game (MicroProse, 1992).
{{< /plate >}}

{{< contributor "C. Michel Boucher" >}}
In the beginning of the game, you will have to travel by land. Except for a few minor exceptions, this is the cheapest, but most time consuming way of travelling.

When travelling across the map, you will encounter all sorts of creatures. The main ones are bandits, boars and wolves, which can all be dispatched. You may also run across tatzelwurms, hellhounds, schrats and the Wild Hunt. If you have a chance to assist a schrat being hunted, do so. He will give you a gift that will be of benefit to you. Any endurance lost during these combats can be recovered in an overnight camp. To recover a fair amount of lost strength, it is best to go to a town and lodge at an inn. The healthy members can earn a little money to help defray the costs while the unhealthy members can recover.
{{< /contributor >}}

In fact, lost Strength comes back in a camp at the same speed as at an inn; the inn is safer, not faster (see [3.15](#315-retiring-characters) for the healing rate). The schrat's gift is a fruit: the character who eats it gains 4 Strength and 4 points of Strength maximum, for good.

### Do horses make the party travel faster?

No. The time each step takes depends only on the terrain. The clue book promises faster travel for a party with a horse for everyone, and the game even works out whether every character is mounted each time the map opens, but it never uses the answer. Horses matter when you want to get away, and when you meet a knight on the road who offers a race or a joust. What they do against wolves, boars and the Wild Hunt is in [5.1](../miscellany/#51-miscellaneous-items) and [4.13](../quests/#413-wild-hunt).

### How often will I meet something on the road?

The longer you march without entering a town, the more often something happens: the chance on each step starts low and rises slowly, up to eleven times its starting value. Rough terrain and the places nearby add to it. Entering a town resets it.

## 3.12 Travelling by Water

{{< plate src="/images/faq/s3-river-ship.png" alt="A sailing ship on a river at sunset, hills behind" side="right" pixel="true" >}}
Arriving by river. Scene picture from the game (MicroProse, 1992).
{{< /plate >}}

{{< contributor "C. Michel Boucher" >}}
Once your wealth improves, you can travel by water. This is costly compared to travelling by land (usually a few groschen), but will get you places much faster (in terms of player time, if not "real" time). For example, you can get from Speyer to Dresden or from Breslau to Flensburg by town hopping. Sometimes, you are lucky and upon arriving at a town, you can just continue on to a further destination without increasing the initial cost of transportation. Sometimes you have to wait a few days by taking on odd jobs (and checking the wharves every day for outgoing ships).
{{< /contributor >}}

### What does a passage cost, and when do ships sail?

<p class="dl-formula">Fare = (city size + 4) pfennigs per character + (city size + 5) per horse<br><small>paid at the port you leave from</small></p>

A party of four without horses pays about three groschen. Each port's ships go to each destination every third day, and the days differ from port to port; a few ports have ships only every fourth day, at twice the fare. After an hour or three of waiting the voyage takes two to four days.

It isn't luck: the arrival wharf offers to sail on to the next ports along the river, free, if a ship is leaving that day. To go ashore you can jump off among the reeds for nothing, pay the wharf toll (a couple of pfennigs per character), talk to the wharfmaster or sneak ashore at night.

## 3.13 Fighting Tactics

{{< plate src="/images/faq/s3-melee.png" alt="A sketch of armoured men fighting with swords, shields and polearms" side="left" pixel="true" >}}
A caravan under attack. Scene picture from the game (MicroProse, 1992).
{{< /plate >}}

{{< contributor "Alexander von Lünen" >}}
This depends on your opponent. While town robbers and cutthroat-thieves may be just wiped away like nothing without any particular strategy, other foes may take a great deal of tactical considerations. First rule is, only hunt what you can kill. Be sure your characters can take care of themselves. If necessary, "train" them by searching out robbers in town. It might be a good idea to put your weaker characters in the first row or even let them fight alone if facing such "training fights". At stronger enemies, even when your party appears strong, you should use all your fighting skills. That means using missile-, bow-, and alchemical-weapons at long-distance and good weapons at mêlée. Use the full bandwidth of your Alchemist. Let him (her) throw potions at longer-distance and use healing potions on your seriously wounded party-members.

The Thunderbolt potion is an excellent all-round alchemical piece (see 3.3), a Stone-Tar potion is of use when you are outnumbered by the enemies and you need to slow down their approach. In buildings or dungeons (mines) put your stronger characters in the first row. Enemies often show up after opening a door. While two of your party (of course the strong ones) block the doorway and engage the foe in mêlée, the rest of your party can shoot at them with their missile weapons. I made a scene:

```
             x
           x   x
---------------------|x x|-----------------
                o  o
              o   o   o

x = Your party    o = Enemies    - = Wall    || = Door
```

With this technique, I managed the Great Monastery quite well, this constellation occurs pretty often there. Another thing of which you should take care, is the "I-haven't-been-harmed-so-I-won't-engage!" attitude of your characters. This means, as long as a party-member hasn't been attacked, he (she) won't attack either. This might not be useful, especially if you're meeting a foe who comes alone, but is hard to fight for one character, like the Wild Hunt or a demon. So make sure, all characters have been assigned to a target.
{{< /contributor >}}

{{< contributor "Fred Farzanegan" >}}
Darkland's excellent battle engine is real-time, but allows you to halt a battle and change strategies on the fly. You are basically a quarterback, giving instructions to your players individually. Your characters are not dumb, however, once they defeat their opponent, they move on to the next. If they are hurt, they try to retreat. This engine is the best I've seen in a game, giving the advantages of both real-time and turn-based combat... the ultimate armchair-warriors battleground!

Fighting strategy. I use two or three sword swingers to intercept attacking enemies. My weaker characters remain in the background and throw darts and potions, fire bows, crossbows, and guns. I also have plenty of stone tar to slow down large groups of opponents. High bow and throwing ability lets you use this strategy well.

Since guns fire so slowly and are so heavy, only my fighters carry them, and fire an initial volley, potentially killing some enemies before the first blow is struck. These swingers then enter the fray, armored well enough to withstand the hand-to-hand onslaught. I try to position them in such a way that no enemies get past them. While this is going on, my back characters with high skills fire between my front-line characters. If a strong opponent gets past the sword-jocks, I lead them on a merry little chase around the battlefield by having my weak characters move around just out of reach. When my fighters have dispatched their unworthy opponents, they intercept the pursuer.

The most important tip I have for fighting is to gang-up on opponents. Try to isolate enemies and beat on them without mercy. The trick is that a fighter can only defend against ONE opponent. The others basically get free shots. What I try to do is get my highest-armored character to pick an opponent and fight normally, and have my weaker characters berserk the enemy who can't defend himself. This allows battles to end very quickly. A modification of this is to have all your bow/gun shooters shoot the same opponent and kill him before he gets into the fray. This is a quick way to even out a fight. As soon as one is killed, begin shooting another. Remember that bow/gun firing characters won't automatically pick the next target- they may begin hand-to-hand, or just mill-around!

Of course, the gang-up strategy can be used by the enemy to quickly take down one of your characters. Beware of being surrounded by enemies.

Fighting with Potions: At the start of a battle, the enemy may be entering the battlefield at a small entrance. Immediately throw several stone-tar potions in his *expected* path, as there's a lag in how quickly your potions get tossed. Always use the highest quality stone-tar (45q). It is MUCH better at slowing down opponents than the others. Once your enemies are stuck, I use exploding and irritation potions to weaken them. NOTE: Be very careful of these potions. If you throw them in the vicinity of your own characters, they will be injured as well.

The above strategies are for slower-moving and human opponents in open areas. Faster moving opponents and tight passages don't really allow these strategies. A single volley may be all that can be fired before engaging the enemy. The only advice is try to gang up on enemies when possible.
{{< /contributor >}}

### How much does ganging up help?

A lot, though not quite as Farzanegan describes: there is no rule that a fighter defends against only one opponent. Instead, each fighter attacking the same enemy hand to hand adds 10 percentage points to every attacker's chance to hit him, and every attacker on you takes 10 points off your own blows. Two on one is a 20-point swing; four on one, 40.

A few more numbers from the game's rules. A berserk attack adds a quarter of the attacker's skill (at least 10) to his chance and strikes faster; a parrying attacker loses a quarter of his skill and strikes slower. Fighting inside a cloud costs 15 points. Thrown potions come in three grades by quality (20 to 29, 30 to 39, 40 and up), and at most eight slowing pools can lie on the field at once: a ninth Stone-tar does nothing. Every cloud and blast strikes whoever stands in it, friend or foe.

### Why are some fights so much bigger than others?

Most fights are sized by your party's "lore level", a rating from 1 to 10:

<p class="dl-formula">(average best weapon skill + best missile skill + average Virtue + average Alchemy) ÷ 37<br><small>−2 for a single character, −1 for two, +1 for five</small></p>

The better your party, the larger the bands of robbers and the groups in the wilderness and the dungeons. A lone character or a pair meets smaller ones.

## 3.15 Retiring Characters

Age matters only when you create a character: it decides which careers are open and what the years of the career cost (see [3.1](#31-character-generation)). In play, each character grows a year older every twelve months, and nothing else happens. No attribute or skill falls with age, so there is no reason to retire anyone at 40, or at 60. Retire a character only because you want a different one, and if you are preparing for a greater mission, think twice before dropping one rich in experience and skill: new characters have to be trained all over again to reach that level.

The README.TXT for v483.07 that comes from Microprose has this to say:

> Characters can temporarily retire at any city inn. You can later return and invite them to rejoin the party once more. However, when anyone joins an existing party, be they an old friend or someone new, they come with NO equipment, just their knowledge. Therefore, before retiring someone, cache their equipment at that inn.
>
> There is no specific screen for retiring the entire party. To retire, just save the game and erase all earlier saved games for that party. You can take them "out of retirement" at any time by loading that saved game.

A character who retires takes a fifth of the party's purse and a fifth of its bank notes, and that money is simply gone. (Because of an original bug, the share is miscounted when the purse holds more than 273 florins.) Hired companions leave without a share. New recruits can join only while fewer than four characters are present; the fifth place is for the hired companions of 3.10.

### How fast do wounds heal?

Endurance comes back in full after a night's rest, in camp or at an inn. Strength is slower: each day spent regaining strength gives back the party's best Healing skill divided by 15 (at least 1 point a day). A healer with Healing 60 gives back 4 points a day to everyone resting. Essence o'Grace is faster (see 3.3).

## 3.16 Post-Baphomet Adventuring

It's possible to keep on playing Darklands after you've accomplished all major and minor tasks, just as it is mentioned in readme.txt of the game. Perhaps you'll simply get tired of doing everything all over again. So one might prefer to start a completely new game and try out some completely new strategy rather than going on in the same way (regardless of the fact that you could create new characters within the running game, retire all the old ones and keep playing with a new party (but old fame) and try new things by this as well).

{{% details title="Spoiler: the end of the game, and what comes after" closed="true" %}}

**Is there an ending?** Yes, a closing film, after Baphomet's last offer: see [4.16](../quests/#416-baphomet). Either way you answer him, the game goes on afterwards. (The manual's Hall of Fame was never put in the game.)

**Does the world reset afterwards?** Partly. Baphomet's castle moves on (see 4.16). Every third month a ruined robber knight's castle somewhere is rebuilt and occupied again. Dragons, mine troubles and witches' sabbats keep starting as before, until the sabbats stop on New Year 1500 ([4.18](../quests/#418-when-and-where-are-the-witches-sabbats)).

## 3.17 Is there a quest log?

No. The game keeps no list of the tasks you have accepted. The Party Information screen (F6) shows your fame, the date, your money and a small map: point at a town on it to see its size and your standing there, and Tab steps through the towns. Write down your commissions, with the patron's town and the place you were sent to.
